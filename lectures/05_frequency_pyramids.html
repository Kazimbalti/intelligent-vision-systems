From Spatial to Frequency Domain

  • Any image can be written as a weighted sum of 2D sinusoids of different frequency, orientation, and phase
  • Slowly-varying regions (sky, skin, a wall) → low spatial frequency
  • Edges, texture, noise → high spatial frequency
  • Filtering in the spatial domain (convolving with a kernel) has an exact equivalent in the frequency domain (multiplying by a mask) — the two views are the same operation, seen differently

The 1D Discrete Fourier Transform

\[ F(u) = \sum_{x=0}^{N-1} f(x)\, e^{-j 2\pi u x / N}, \qquad u = 0,\dots,N-1 \]

  • \(F(u)\) is complex: magnitude \(|F(u)|\) (how much of frequency \(u\) is present) and phase \(\angle F(u)\) (where it is located)
  • Inverse DFT reconstructs \(f(x)\) exactly from \(F(u)\) — no information is lost
  • \(u=0\) is the DC term — the mean value of the signal

The 2D DFT

\[ F(u,v) = \sum_{x=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1} f(x,y)\, e^{-j2\pi\left(\frac{ux}{M}+\frac{vy}{N}\right)} \]

  • Separable in \(x\) and \(y\) — compute the 2D DFT as an FFT of every row, then an FFT of every column
  • \((u,v)\) index 2D spatial frequency: distance from the origin = frequency magnitude, angle = orientation
  • Images are real-valued, so the spectrum is conjugate-symmetric: \(F(-u,-v) = F^*(u,v)\)

The FFT — Why We Never Compute the DFT Directly

  • Direct DFT evaluation: \(O(N^2)\) per dimension — for a 1080p frame, computationally infeasible in real time
  • Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) (Cooley–Tukey, 1965): exploits the recursive structure of the DFT to compute it in \(O(N \log N)\)
  • Fastest when \(N\) is a power of two (or has small prime factors) — np.fft.fft2/cv2.dft pad or choose sizes accordingly for best performance
import numpy as np
F = np.fft.fft2(gray)          # complex spectrum
Fshift = np.fft.fftshift(F)    # move the DC term to the centre for display

Reading a Spectrum

  • Display log magnitude, not raw magnitude — the DC term and a few low frequencies otherwise dwarf everything else on a linear scale: \(\;20\log_{10}(1+|F(u,v)|)\)
  • After fftshift, the centre of the display is the DC term (low frequency); distance from centre = frequency magnitude
  • A bright horizontal line in the spectrum → strong vertical structure in the image (and vice versa) — the spectrum orientation is rotated 90° from the spatial-domain structure that causes it
  • Isolated bright dots off-centre → a periodic pattern in the image (screen-door effect, scan-line interference, printer halftoning)
mag = 20 * np.log(np.abs(Fshift) + 1)
mag_u8 = cv2.normalize(mag, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX).astype(np.uint8)

The Convolution Theorem

Convolution in the spatial domain is pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain:

\[ f * h \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; F \cdot H \]

  • \(\mathcal{F}\{f * h\} = \mathcal{F}\{f\}\cdot \mathcal{F}\{h\}\), and symmetrically \(\mathcal{F}\{f \cdot h\} = \mathcal{F}\{f\} * \mathcal{F}\{h\}\)
  • This is why smoothing = removing high-frequency content, and sharpening = boosting it — it is exactly what the theorem predicts
  • Practical payoff: for a large kernel \(h\) (radius > ~15–20 px), it is often faster to FFT both \(f\) and \(h\), multiply, and inverse-FFT than to convolve directly in the spatial domain

Designing Frequency-Domain Filters

A frequency filter is a mask \(H(u,v)\) multiplied onto the shifted spectrum:

  1. Compute \(F = \text{fftshift}(\text{fft2}(f))\)
  2. Multiply: \(G = F \cdot H\)
  3. Invert: \(g = \text{real}(\text{ifft2}(\text{ifftshift}(G)))\)

Three common shapes for \(H\), each available as low-pass, high-pass, or band-pass:

Ideal Filters

A hard cutoff at radius \(D_0\) from the centre:

\[ H_{\text{lp}}(u,v) = \begin{cases} 1 & D(u,v) \le D_0 \\ 0 & D(u,v) > D_0 \end{cases} \]

  • Conceptually the simplest filter — and the worst-behaved in practice
  • A sharp cutoff in frequency corresponds to a wide, oscillating sinc-shaped kernel in the spatial domain → produces visible ringing around edges (see the ringing slide below)

Butterworth Filters

A smooth roll-off controlled by order \(n\):

\[ H_{\text{lp}}(u,v) = \frac{1}{1 + \left(D(u,v)/D_0\right)^{2n}} \]

  • \(n=1\): very gentle roll-off, minimal ringing, weak frequency separation
  • Large \(n\): approaches the ideal filter's sharp cutoff (and its ringing)
  • Good middle ground between the ideal filter (sharp, ringy) and the Gaussian filter (smooth, no sharp control) — \(n=2\) is a common default

Gaussian Frequency Filters

\[ H_{\text{lp}}(u,v) = e^{-D(u,v)^2 / 2D_0^2} \]

  • A Gaussian in the frequency domain has a Gaussian in the spatial domain too (the Gaussian is its own Fourier transform, up to scaling) — no ringing whatsoever
  • High-pass version: \(H_{\text{hp}} = 1 - H_{\text{lp}}\)
  • This is the theoretical justification for why cv2.GaussianBlur (Lecture 4) is the default smoothing choice: it is the smoothest possible frequency cutoff

Band-Pass & Notch Filtering

  • Band-pass: combine a low-pass and a high-pass mask (\(H = H_{\text{lp},D_0} \cdot H_{\text{hp},D_1}\), \(D_1 < D_0\)) to keep only a ring of mid frequencies — isolates texture at a specific scale
  • Band-reject / notch: zero out a small region of the spectrum at a specific \((u_0,v_0)\) (and its symmetric partner) to remove a periodic interference pattern — e.g. mains-frequency scan-line noise, a moiré pattern from a display or mesh
  • Workflow: inspect the log-magnitude spectrum, find the tell-tale bright dot(s) away from the centre, null them (and their mirror image), inverse transform

Ringing (Gibbs Phenomenon)

  • A sharp cutoff in one domain produces oscillation in the other — this is the Gibbs phenomenon, the same effect that causes overshoot when approximating a square wave with a truncated Fourier series
  • Visually: faint parallel “echoes” or halos parallel to strong edges after ideal low/high-pass filtering
  • Fix: use a smoothly-tapering filter (Butterworth with moderate order, or Gaussian) instead of a hard cutoff — the same lesson as choosing kernel shapes in Lecture 4

Practitioner Notes — Frequency Domain

  • FFT-based filtering pays off for large kernels; for the 3×3–15×15 kernels used in Lectures 3–4, direct spatial convolution is faster — don't reach for the FFT by default
  • Always fftshift before visualising/designing a filter mask, and ifftshift before inverse-transforming — forgetting either silently corrupts the result
  • Notch filtering requires the noise to be strictly periodic and visible as a compact spot in the spectrum; broadband noise (most sensor noise) will not respond to it
  • On the Raspberry Pi 5, prefer spatial-domain filters for the live camera loop; use FFT analysis offline / for diagnosis (e.g. discovering a rolling-shutter or PWM-lighting artefact)

The 2D Sampling Theorem

A continuous signal band-limited to frequency \(f_{\max}\) can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at or above the Nyquist rate:

\[ f_s \ge 2 f_{\max} \]

  • In imaging: the sensor's pixel pitch sets \(f_s\); any scene detail finer than the Nyquist limit cannot be captured correctly
  • Directly connects to Lecture 2: this is why a Bayer sensor needs an (often deliberately slightly soft) optical low-pass / anti-aliasing filter in front of it

Aliasing

  • When \(f_s < 2f_{\max}\), high frequencies masquerade as lower, incorrect frequencies in the sampled signal — they fold back ("alias") into the visible spectrum
  • Classic visual symptoms: moiré patterns on fine fabric or mesh, jagged/staircased diagonal edges, wagon-wheel effect in video (temporal aliasing of a rotating spoke pattern)
  • Downsampling an image (resizing smaller) is a new, coarser sampling of the same signal — it can introduce aliasing if done naively

Anti-Aliasing

Rule: low-pass filter before you downsample, so that no content above the new Nyquist limit survives to fold back.

# Wrong: naive nearest/area resize on a high-frequency image can alias
small_bad = cv2.resize(img, (w // 4, h // 4), interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)

# Right: blur to the new Nyquist limit, then resize (or use INTER_AREA, which
# effectively does local averaging and is the safe default for shrinking)
blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (5, 5), 0)
small_ok = cv2.resize(blurred, (w // 4, h // 4), interpolation=cv2.INTER_AREA)

This exact pre-blur-then-subsample step is also the construction rule for a Gaussian pyramid, next.

The Gaussian Pyramid

A multi-resolution representation: repeatedly blur and downsample by 2×.

\[ G_0 = f, \qquad G_{l+1} = \operatorname{downsample}_2\!\big(G_l * G_\sigma\big) \]

  • Each level is half the width and height of the level below — a stack of images from full resolution down to a few pixels
  • The Gaussian pre-blur at every level is exactly the anti-aliasing step above — skip it and the pyramid aliases badly
  • Used for multi-scale search (find a face/object at an unknown size by scanning every level), and for coarse-to-fine algorithms (below)
G1 = cv2.pyrDown(img)   # blur + downsample by 2, one pyramid level
G2 = cv2.pyrDown(G1)

The Laplacian Pyramid

Stores the detail lost between consecutive Gaussian-pyramid levels, enabling exact reconstruction:

\[ L_l = G_l - \operatorname{upsample}_2\!\big(G_{l+1}\big) \]

  • Each \(L_l\) is a band-pass image: the detail present at level \(l\) but not at the coarser level \(l+1\) — conceptually a discretised, multi-scale version of the Laplacian-of-Gaussian from Lecture 4
  • The original image is recovered exactly by adding the residuals back while upsampling: \(G_l = L_l + \operatorname{upsample}_2(G_{l+1})\)
  • Classic application: seamless multi-band image blending (blend each frequency band separately, avoiding a visible seam at any single scale) — e.g. for panorama stitching in Lecture 8
up = cv2.pyrUp(G1, dstsize=(G0.shape[1], G0.shape[0]))
L0 = cv2.subtract(G0, up)   # Laplacian residual at the finest level

Coarse-to-Fine Processing

  • Solve a hard problem at the coarsest pyramid level first (cheap, and large motions/displacements look small relative to the tiny image), then refine the estimate at each successively finer level
  • This single idea underlies:
    • Pyramidal Lucas–Kanade optical flow (Lecture 11) — track large motions that a single-scale KLT tracker would miss entirely
    • Multi-scale template/object search — find candidates coarse, localise precisely fine
    • Coarse-to-fine stereo matching — bound the disparity search range using the coarse level's estimate
  • General pattern: estimate → warp/refine → move down one pyramid level → repeat

Key Takeaways

  • Spatial filtering (Lecture 4) and frequency filtering are the same operation — the convolution theorem is the bridge, and it explains why Gaussian filters are smooth and ideal filters ring
  • The Nyquist rate sets a hard limit on what a sensor or a downsampling step can capture correctly; anti-alias (pre-blur) before you subsample, always
  • Gaussian and Laplacian pyramids give you multi-resolution image representations for free, built from a single repeated blur-and-downsample rule
  • Coarse-to-fine is a general algorithmic strategy, not just a pyramid trick — you will reuse it directly for optical flow in Lecture 11
