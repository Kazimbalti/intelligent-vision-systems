Why Restoration?

Every acquired image is a degraded version of an ideal scene. The standard degradation model:

\[ g(x,y) = h(x,y) * f(x,y) + n(x,y) \]

  • \(f\) — the ideal, noise-free image
  • \(h\) — a (possibly unknown) degradation/blur kernel, applied by convolution
  • \(n\) — additive noise, independent of the signal
  • \(g\) — what the sensor actually delivers

Restoration tries to invert this model and recover \(f\) from \(g\) — as opposed to enhancement (Lecture 3–4), which improves appearance without a formal degradation model.

Gaussian & Salt-and-Pepper Noise

Gaussian: \(g = f + n,\ n \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) — additive, signal-independent, and the noise most classical filters are designed around.

Salt-and-pepper (impulse): a fraction \(p\) of pixels are forced to \(0\) or \(255\); a linear (mean/Gaussian) filter spreads the outliers instead of removing them — this is exactly why the median filter (a non-linear, order-statistic filter) is the right tool: it replaces each pixel with the median of its neighbourhood, which is robust to outliers.

import numpy as np, cv2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
gauss = img.astype(np.float32) + rng.normal(0, 15, img.shape)
gauss = np.clip(gauss, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)

sp = img.copy()
mask = rng.random(img.shape[:2])
sp[mask < 0.02] = 0        # pepper
sp[mask > 0.98] = 255      # salt

denoised_gauss = cv2.GaussianBlur(gauss, (5, 5), 0)
denoised_sp    = cv2.medianBlur(sp, 5)

Poisson & Speckle Noise

  • Poisson / shot noise: photon arrivals are a counting process, so variance grows with intensity: \(\mathrm{Var}[g] \approx f\). Dominant in low-light and scientific/medical imaging.
    • Trick: the Anscombe transform \(z = 2\sqrt{f + 3/8}\) approximately turns Poisson noise into unit-variance Gaussian noise, so a standard Gaussian denoiser can be applied in \(z\)-space, then inverted.
  • Speckle noise: multiplicative, \(g = f\cdot(1+n)\); arises from coherent-wave interference (ultrasound, SAR, laser scanners). Log-transform turns it into additive noise before filtering (Lee, Frost, or NLM in the log domain).

Inverse Filtering

If we know (or estimate) the blur kernel \(h\), the degradation model in the frequency domain is \(G = HF\) (convolution → multiplication). The naive inverse filter:

\[ \hat{F}(u,v) = \frac{G(u,v)}{H(u,v)} \]

  • Works perfectly with no noise: \(\hat F = F\) exactly.
  • Catastrophic with real noise: wherever \(H(u,v)\) is small (most blur kernels are low-pass, so \(H\) is small at high frequencies), dividing amplifies noise without bound.
  • This is why inverse filtering is a textbook stepping stone, not a practical tool — it motivates the Wiener filter.

Wiener Filtering

The Wiener filter is the minimum mean-square-error linear estimator of \(f\) given \(g\), assuming known signal and noise power spectra:

\[ \hat{F}(u,v) = \left[\frac{1}{H(u,v)}\; \frac{|H(u,v)|^2}{|H(u,v)|^2 + K}\right] G(u,v) \]

  • \(K \approx S_n(u,v)/S_f(u,v)\) — the noise-to-signal power ratio (often tuned as a single scalar in practice).
  • As \(K \to 0\) (no noise): Wiener → the inverse filter.
  • As \(K \to \infty\) (all noise, no signal): the filter output → 0, suppressing the amplification problem.
  • The Wiener filter is optimal only under the (often wrong) assumption that the image and noise are wide-sense-stationary Gaussian processes — a reasonable first approximation, not a universal solution.

Non-Local Means (NLM)

Classical filters (Gaussian, median) only look at a small local neighbourhood. NLM instead searches the whole image (or a large search window) for patches that look similar to the patch around the current pixel, and averages across them:

\[ \mathrm{NL}[f](x) = \sum_{y \in \Omega} w(x,y)\, f(y), \qquad w(x,y) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert P(x) - P(y) \rVert_2^2}{h^2}\right) \]

  • \(P(x)\)/\(P(y)\) are small patches (e.g. 7×7) centred at \(x\)/\(y\); \(h\) controls how strict the similarity match is.
  • Exploits self-similarity: natural images repeat texture/edges elsewhere in the frame — averaging many independent noisy observations of the “same” patch cancels noise while preserving detail.
denoised = cv2.fastNlMeansDenoisingColored(
    img, None, h=10, hColor=10,
    templateWindowSize=7, searchWindowSize=21)

Total-Variation (TV) Denoising

TV denoising treats restoration as an optimisation problem: find \(u\) close to the noisy image \(g\) but with small total gradient magnitude (piecewise-smooth):

\[ \hat{u} = \arg\min_{u}\; \underbrace{\int |\nabla u|\, dx}_{\text{TV regulariser}} \;+\; \lambda \underbrace{\lVert u - g \rVert_2^2}_{\text{data fidelity}} \]

  • The \(L_1\)-like TV term penalises gradients everywhere, but unlike Gaussian smoothing it does not penalise a single large jump (an edge) as heavily as many small ones — so TV denoising removes noise while keeping edges sharp (“staircasing” artefact aside).
  • \(\lambda\) trades off smoothness vs. fidelity to the observed image; solved iteratively (gradient descent, primal-dual / Chambolle–Pock, ADMM).
  • Conceptual ancestor of modern learned denoisers and diffusion-model priors.

Quantifying Quality: PSNR & SSIM

Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (higher is better; measured against a ground-truth/reference image):

\[ \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{MN}\sum_{i,j}\big(f(i,j)-\hat f(i,j)\big)^2, \qquad \mathrm{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{255^2}{\mathrm{MSE}}\right)\ \text{dB} \]

Structural Similarity (SSIM) compares local luminance, contrast, and structure instead of raw pixel error — it correlates much better with human perception than PSNR/MSE:

\[ \mathrm{SSIM}(x,y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2+\mu_y^2+C_1)(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2+C_2)} \]

from skimage.metrics import peak_signal_noise_ratio, structural_similarity
psnr = peak_signal_noise_ratio(clean, denoised)
ssim = structural_similarity(clean, denoised, channel_axis=2)

Practitioner note: a high PSNR does not guarantee a visually pleasing result (over-smoothing can raise PSNR while destroying texture) — always eyeball crops alongside the numbers.

Restoration — Practitioner Notes

  • Match the filter to the noise model — a median filter barely touches Gaussian noise; a Gaussian blur barely touches salt-and-pepper.
  • Denoise before downstream operations (edge detection, thresholding) whose outputs are noise-sensitive, not after.
  • NLM and TV are expensive — on a Raspberry Pi 5, prefer medianBlur/bilateralFilter/GaussianBlur for real-time video and reserve NLM for offline/still-image cleanup.
  • Always validate on a held-out reference: PSNR/SSIM without a ground-truth image degrades to “it looks smoother,” which is not a metric.

From Pixels to Shapes

Restoration cleans up intensity values. Morphology operates on the shape of regions in a binary (or grayscale) image — the natural next step after thresholding.

Binary Images & Structuring Elements

Morphological operators probe a binary image \(A\) with a small shape \(B\), the structuring element (SE) — analogous to a convolution kernel, but the operation is set-based, not a weighted sum.

se_rect  = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_RECT,    (5, 5))
se_ellip = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (5, 5))
se_cross = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_CROSS,   (5, 5))
  • SE shape/size sets what “connectedness” and scale the operator responds to — a 3×3 SE removes single-pixel noise; a 15×15 SE closes larger gaps.
  • An anchor point (usually the SE centre) defines where the result is written.

Erosion & Dilation

Erosion — a pixel survives only if the whole SE fits inside the foreground:

\[ A \ominus B = \{\, z \mid B_z \subseteq A \,\} \]

Dilation — a pixel is added if the SE hits the foreground anywhere:

\[ A \oplus B = \{\, z \mid (\hat B_z \cap A) \neq \emptyset \,\} \]

  • Erosion shrinks foreground blobs and removes small isolated specks; dilation grows blobs and fills small holes.
  • They are duals under complement: \((A \ominus B)^c = A^c \oplus \hat B\).
eroded  = cv2.erode(mask, se_ellip, iterations=1)
dilated = cv2.dilate(mask, se_ellip, iterations=1)

Opening & Closing

Opening (erosion then dilation) — removes small foreground specks and thin protrusions while roughly preserving the size of large objects:

\[ A \circ B = (A \ominus B) \oplus B \]

Closing (dilation then erosion) — fills small holes and gaps, joining nearby blobs:

\[ A \bullet B = (A \oplus B) \ominus B \]

opened = cv2.morphologyEx(mask, cv2.MORPH_OPEN,  se_ellip)
closed = cv2.morphologyEx(mask, cv2.MORPH_CLOSE, se_ellip)

Rule of thumb: noisy speckled mask → open first; broken/fragmented mask → close first. Labs 6 and later chain both, exactly as in applied-cv-course/code/02_filters_edges.py (MORPH_OPEN then MORPH_CLOSE).

Hit-or-Miss Transform

A generalisation that matches an exact local pattern using two structuring elements — a foreground SE \(B_1\) that must hit, and a background SE \(B_2\) that must miss:

\[ A \circledast (B_1,B_2) = (A \ominus B_1) \cap (A^c \ominus B_2) \]

  • Used to find specific shapes — corners, endpoints of a skeleton, isolated single pixels.
  • OpenCV: cv2.morphologyEx(mask, cv2.MORPH_HITMISS, kernel) with a kernel containing 1 (foreground), -1 (background), 0 (don’t care).

Connected-Component Labelling

After cleanup, we usually want to enumerate and measure the individual blobs, not just the mask as a whole.

n_labels, labels, stats, centroids = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(
    closed, connectivity=8)
for i in range(1, n_labels):                 # skip label 0 = background
    x, y, w, h, area = stats[i]
    cx, cy = centroids[i]
    if area < 50:
        continue                             # discard tiny specks
    cv2.rectangle(out, (x, y), (x + w, y + h), (0, 255, 0), 2)
  • 4-connectivity (N/S/E/W neighbours) vs 8-connectivity (+ diagonals) changes what counts as “one blob” — 8-connectivity is the common default for object counting.
  • stats gives bounding box + area per label directly — the fastest path to a size/count measurement (this is exactly Lab 9’s object-size pipeline).

Distance Transform

For every foreground pixel, the distance transform computes the distance to the nearest background pixel — producing a grayscale “height map” that peaks at the centre of thick regions:

dist = cv2.distanceTransform(mask, cv2.DIST_L2, maskSize=5)
_, sure_fg = cv2.threshold(dist, 0.5 * dist.max(), 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)
  • Local maxima of the distance map are good candidates for object centres / seed points.
  • Core building block for the watershed segmentation algorithm covered in Lecture 9 — the distance transform supplies the “sure foreground” markers.

Skeletonisation / Thinning

Reduces a blob to a 1-pixel-wide medial axis that preserves topology (branches, loops, endpoints) while discarding thickness:

  • Classical algorithm: repeatedly erode with hit-or-miss templates that remove boundary pixels without breaking connectivity, until no more pixels can be removed (Zhang–Suen thinning is a common practical version).
  • Applications: fingerprint ridge extraction, handwriting/stroke analysis, road-network extraction from aerial masks, robot path-planning corridors.
  • OpenCV: cv2.ximgproc.thinning(mask) (contrib module) or skimage.morphology.skeletonize.

Morphology — Practitioner Notes

  • SE size is a scale parameter — too small and noise survives; too large and thin real structures (wires, text strokes) are erased.
  • Order matters: open-then-close vs close-then-open give different results on the same mask — try both.
  • Morphology assumes a reasonably clean binary mask; garbage in from a bad threshold means garbage out no matter how much morphology is applied — fix the threshold step first.
  • All of these operators run in native OpenCV C++ and are effectively free on the Pi 5 — they are the cheapest way to clean a detector/segmenter’s output before measurement.

Putting It Together: A Clean Pipeline

gray    = cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(gray, (5, 5), 0)
_, th   = cv2.threshold(blurred, 0, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)

kernel  = cv2.getStructuringElement(cv2.MORPH_ELLIPSE, (5, 5))
opened  = cv2.morphologyEx(th, cv2.MORPH_OPEN,  kernel)
closed  = cv2.morphologyEx(opened, cv2.MORPH_CLOSE, kernel)

n, labels, stats, centroids = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(closed, connectivity=8)

This exact denoise → threshold → open → close → label chain is the backbone of Lab 6 and reappears throughout the course (defect detection in Lab 9, contour features in Lab 5).

Key Takeaways

  • Restoration models degradation explicitly (\(g = h*f+n\)) and tries to invert it; enhancement does not.
  • Match the denoiser to the noise model: median for impulse noise, Wiener/Gaussian for additive Gaussian noise, NLM/TV when quality matters more than speed.
  • PSNR/SSIM give you a number to defend a claim of “better” — always report them against a ground truth.
  • Erosion/dilation/opening/closing are the four fundamental shape operators; almost every practical morphology recipe is built from them.
  • Connected components + distance transform turn a clean binary mask into measurements (Lab 9 leans directly on this).
