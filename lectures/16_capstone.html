Today's Format

  • No new lecture content this week — this session is capstone demos + final-exam review.
  • Every student demonstrates their Lab Project (capstone) live on Raspberry Pi 5 target hardware, gives a short talk, takes Q&A, and receives peer review.
  • The back half of the session is a structured walk through everything the Final Exam (40 marks, finals week) can cover.

Demo-Day Logistics: Schedule & Format

  • Each team/student gets a fixed slot (announced in advance): set-up → short talk → live demo → Q&A → peer review, timed strictly so every slot in the 1.5-hour lab session gets a fair share.
  • Set-up happens before your slot starts — have the Pi 5 powered, network/peripherals connected, and the application already running in the background if boot time is long.
  • If your slot is early, be ready before session start; if it is late, keep your demo running/stable in the meantime rather than restarting it.

What to Bring / Bring-Up Checklist

  • The Raspberry Pi 5 with your project pre-installed, plus power supply, camera, and any accessories (AI HAT/Hailo, ESP32-CAM, monitor/HDMI or a laptop for remote display).
  • A charged laptop with your codebase and slides, in case the Pi needs last-minute debugging.
  • A backup screen recording of a successful run — live demos fail sometimes (network, lighting, hardware); a backup video lets you still show working results and keeps grading fair.
  • Your written project report, printed or ready to open instantly.

Structuring Your Short Talk

  1. Problem — what task does your system solve, and why does it matter?
  2. Approach — which pipeline/model/tier did you choose, and why (tie back to the Appendix C tier-comparison thinking: ESP32 vs Pi CPU vs Pi+Hailo)?
  3. Architecture — capture → inference → tracking/logic → action/output, in one diagram or sentence.
  4. Live demo — run it on the actual Pi 5, on a live or realistic input.
  5. Results — report accuracy/mAP/IoU (whichever applies) and FPS/latency/power on-device, not just on a laptop.
  6. Limitations — what breaks it, and what you would fix with more time.

Keep the talk tight; most of your slot is demo + Q&A, not slides.

Lab Project Grading Rubric

Of the 30-mark Lab Project (capstone) component:

CriterionWeight
Technical difficulty & correctness30%
Runs on the Raspberry Pi 5 in real time (or a justified budget)25%
Experimental rigour — baselines, ablations, honest metrics20%
Report clarity & reproducibility15%
Presentation & demo10%

“Runs in real time” means measured, on-device FPS/latency — not a number from a laptop or Colab.

Peer-Review Guidance: What to Ask

  • Is the claimed accuracy/FPS measured on the Pi 5, on a held-out set, honestly reported (including failure cases)?
  • Does the demo generalise beyond the exact clip/scene it was tuned on?
  • What is the action layer — does a detection actually change what the system does (log, alert, actuate), or does the pipeline stop at "detect and display"?
  • What would break this system in a different lighting condition, camera angle, or with more objects/people in frame?
  • Give one specific, actionable piece of feedback — peer review only helps if it is concrete.

Common Demo-Day Failure Modes & Mitigations

FailureMitigation
Wi-Fi/network drops mid-demoPrefer a local display or a wired connection; have the backup recording ready
Lighting in the demo room differs from your training dataTest in the actual room beforehand; bring a portable light if your system is lighting-sensitive
Model loads slowly / long cold-startStart the process before your slot; warm up the camera and first inference in advance
Hailo/accelerator not detectedRe-run hailortcli identify before your slot; have a CPU-only fallback path
Live camera has nothing interesting in frameBring a physical prop / phone video to guarantee a detectable target

Final Exam Review: Weeks 1–4

Classical fundamentals — know the definitions, the pipeline, and the key formulas:

  • Week 1: the intelligent-vision-system pipeline (acquire → process → analyse → decide → act); radiometry, lenses, aperture, depth of field; pinhole/thin-lens models; perspective projection; exposure & motion blur.
  • Week 2: CCD vs CMOS; Bayer CFA & demosaicing; the ISP pipeline; rolling shutter; sampling & quantisation; colour spaces (RGB, HSV, YCbCr, Lab).
  • Week 3: LUTs, gamma/log maps, histogram equalisation/CLAHE; global/Otsu/adaptive/Sauvola thresholding; image arithmetic & blending.
  • Week 4: correlation vs convolution, separable kernels, integral images; sharpening/Laplacian; gradients (Sobel/Prewitt/Scharr); median/bilateral/guided filters.

Final Exam Review: Weeks 5–9

  • Week 5: 1D/2D DFT, the convolution theorem, ideal/Butterworth/Gaussian filters; sampling theorem, aliasing, Gaussian/Laplacian pyramids.
  • Week 6: noise models; inverse/Wiener filtering, PSNR/SSIM; erosion/dilation/opening/closing, connected components, skeletonisation.
  • Week 7: Canny in depth, Hough transform, moments/Hu invariants; Harris/Shi–Tomasi corners, DoG blobs, FAST.
  • Week 8: SIFT/SURF/ORB/BRIEF, ratio test, FLANN vs brute-force; homography by DLT, RANSAC/MSAC, panorama stitching.
  • Week 9: region growing, k-means/mean-shift, SLIC, IoU/Dice; watershed, GrabCut, active contours; the bridge to learned segmentation.

Final Exam Review: Weeks 10–12

  • Week 10: intrinsics/extrinsics, distortion models, Zhang's calibration, reprojection error; epipolar geometry, fundamental/essential matrices, SGBM disparity, depth from disparity.
  • Week 11: brightness constancy, Lucas–Kanade, Horn–Schunck, pyramidal KLT; MOG2/KNN background subtraction, the Kalman filter, KCF/CSRT, SORT, MOT metrics.
  • Week 12: sliding-window detection, HOG+SVM, Haar cascades, bag-of-visual-words; perceptron → MLP, back-propagation, SGD/momentum/Adam, regularisation & augmentation.

The exam is weighted toward weeks 10–16 — make sure these three weeks and the ones below are solid.

Final Exam Review: Weeks 13–15

  • Week 13: convolution & pooling, receptive fields, batch norm; the LeNet→ResNet→MobileNet→EfficientNet lineage; two-stage vs one-stage detection; FCN/U-Net/DeepLab/Mask R-CNN; mAP; the 5-step paper-reading method.
  • Week 14: accelerators (GPU/NPU/DSP/FPGA); TensorRT/TFLite/ONNX Runtime/OpenVINO/Hailo; PTQ vs QAT, INT8/FP16, pruning, distillation; OpenCV 5.0's new DNN engine, breaking changes, and migration strategy.
  • Week 15: scaled dot-product & multi-head attention, positional encoding, the ViT patch/[CLS]/position-embedding pipeline; MAE and contrastive self-supervision; CLIP's contrastive loss and zero-shot use; event cameras; a SLAM overview; ethics/privacy/bias/safety.

Where to Go Next

  • Follow-on courses/topics: robotics & SLAM, advanced deep learning, reinforcement learning, embedded systems / real-time systems.
  • Keep reading papers using the 5-step method from Lecture 13 — it works on any architecture, not just U-Net and the Transformer.
  • Contribute to open source: OpenCV, Ultralytics YOLO, Hugging Face — real-world codebases built by exactly this kind of applied CV work.
  • Challenge tier: revisit the Advanced Project Bank's challenge-tier ideas (a custom visual-classification feature, video-based detection monitoring, a full end-to-end custom vision system) as a next personal project, beyond the graded capstone.
